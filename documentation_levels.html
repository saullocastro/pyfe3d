How the documentation is organised#

The documentation of pyfe3d is written in four cumulative levels, each with its own place, so that a reader, or a tool, can go from what a function does to why it does it without the levels repeating each other.

Level

Where

Content

1, functional

docstrings of the modules, classes, functions and methods, except their Notes and References sections; comments in the code; docstrings and comments of the tests, except their Notes and References; the README.md

what each resource does: a one-line summary, an extended summary (what, not why), Parameters with type, unit, shape and default, Returns, Raises and See Also

2, theory

the Notes and References sections of the docstrings of the library and of the tests, used only for theory

the theory that each resource implements, local to it: the governing equations in the notation of the code, the assumptions and limits of validity, the meaning of each parameter in that context, the algorithm, and the published sources

3, theory in a larger context

the pages of this documentation, under “User guide” and “Analyses and solvers”

the theory written like a book: how the pieces of level 2 fit together, the workflow of an analysis, the conventions shared by the modules, the choice of element, with links to the API instead of repeating level 2

4, worked examples

the Jupyter notebooks in notebooks/ at the root of the repository, rendered on this site under “Worked examples” and “Tutorials”

complete analyses, mostly benchmarks reproduced from the literature, with stored outputs and the results compared with their reference values

The API pages render levels 1 and 2 together. To find the theory of a resource, read the Notes of its docstring; to find how a method fits in an analysis, read the pages of level 3, starting from Overview and workflow.

The tests#

The docstrings and comments of the tests in tests/ are part of level 1, their Notes and References of level 2; the tests are also usage examples, see Repository structure. Each test states in its docstring what it verifies and against what:

  • a published value, a table or an equation of the literature, cited with its location, e.g. the buckling load of a simply supported plate of Leissa (1969), Eq. (10.46);

  • a closed-form solution written in the test, with its source;

  • a property that holds for any input, e.g. the invariance of a buckling load under a rigid rotation of the model, the rank of an element matrix, or a patch test.

When a finite element result is compared with the literature, the tolerance is that of the discretisation, measured by refining the mesh and stated in the docstring, or one of the grades of MacNeal and Harder (1985), and not a value fitted to the output of the code. When the literature is matched only on a mesh too expensive for a test, the docstring records the runs of the same test with a larger refinement that match it, and the test compares its coarse mesh with the value that the same test gave on that mesh, with a tolerance for round-off only, keeping a check against the literature within a grade.
