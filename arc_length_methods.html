Arc-length methods#

Under load control, the load factor of each increment is prescribed, and the Newton-Raphson iterations of The Newton-Raphson method fail at a limit point of the load, where the tangent stiffness matrix is singular and no equilibrium exists near the current configuration for a larger load. The arc-length methods make the load factor an unknown of each increment, together with the configuration, and add one equation that fixes the length of the increment along the equilibrium path. They trace limit points, snap-through and snap-back. Analysis.NL_method = 'arc_length_riks' and 'arc_length_crisfield' select the methods of Riks (1979) and Crisfield (1981), which share the implementation of pyfe3d.solver.arc_length._solver_arc_length() and differ only in how the correction of the load factor is computed.

The arc-length constraint#

With \(\Delta \boldsymbol{u}\) and \(\Delta\lambda\) the increments of the configuration and of the load factor from the last converged point, the step satisfies, besides the equilibrium \(\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{u} + \Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \lambda + \Delta\lambda) = \boldsymbol{0}\), the constraint

(1)#\[\frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{u}^\top \Delta \boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{u}_{ref}^\top \boldsymbol{u}_{ref}} + \psi^2 \Delta\lambda^2 = \Delta s^2\]

with \(\Delta s\) the arc length of the step, \(\psi\) = PSI \(= 1\), and \(\boldsymbol{u}_{ref} = \boldsymbol{K}_{C0}^{-1} \boldsymbol{F}_{ext}\) the linear solution of the reference load, which makes \(\Delta s\) dimensionless and independent of a uniform change of the units of the displacements, though not of a change of the units of the rotations only. In the linear regime, \(\Delta \boldsymbol{u} = \Delta\lambda \, \boldsymbol{u}_{ref}\) and \(\Delta s = \sqrt{1 + \psi^2} \, \Delta\lambda\). This is the spherical constraint of Crisfield (1991, Sec. 9.3.2, Eq. (9.19)), whose displacement term is here scaled and whose load term, there \(\psi^2 \Delta\lambda^2 \boldsymbol{F}_{ext}^\top \boldsymbol{F}_{ext}\), is not. It is convenient to write it with the inner product of the space of the configurations and the load factor,

(2)#\[\langle (\boldsymbol{a}, \alpha), (\boldsymbol{b}, \beta) \rangle = \frac{\boldsymbol{a}^\top \boldsymbol{b}}{\boldsymbol{u}_{ref}^\top \boldsymbol{u}_{ref}} + \psi^2 \alpha \beta, \qquad g = \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \langle (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda), (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda) \rangle - \Delta s^2 \right) = 0\]

When \(\boldsymbol{u}_{ref}\) is null, its squared norm is replaced by 1; when calc_fext declares lbd, e.g. under prescribed displacements, \(\boldsymbol{u}_{ref}\) is the linear solution of \(-\partial \boldsymbol{R}/\partial \lambda\) at \(\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}\), \(\lambda = 0\), see Load- and configuration-dependent loads.

Predictor#

Each step starts from the tangent at the last converged point, \(\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q = \boldsymbol{K}_T^{-1} \boldsymbol{q}\), with \(\boldsymbol{q} = -\partial \boldsymbol{R}/\partial \lambda\), equal to \(\boldsymbol{F}_{ext}\) for a load that depends neither on the configuration nor on the load factor:

(3)#\[\Delta\lambda = \pm \frac{\Delta s}{\sqrt{\langle (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1) \rangle}}, \qquad \Delta \boldsymbol{u} = \Delta\lambda \, \delta \boldsymbol{u}_q\]

which satisfies the constraint (1). The sign is that of \(\langle (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1), (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}_{prev}, \Delta\lambda_{prev}) \rangle\), with the increment of the previous step, positive at the first step: the predictor continues in the direction of the path already traced, which makes \(\Delta\lambda\) negative after a limit point of the load. The first arc length, \(\Delta s_0 =\) an.initialInc \(\sqrt{\langle (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1) \rangle}\) at the origin, corresponds to a load factor increment of an.initialInc along the initial tangent.

Corrections#

At each iteration, the residual is \(\boldsymbol{R} = \boldsymbol{F}_{int}(\boldsymbol{u} + \Delta \boldsymbol{u}) - (\lambda + \Delta\lambda) \boldsymbol{F}_{ext}\), and the linearized equilibrium is the bordered system \(\boldsymbol{K}_T \delta \boldsymbol{u} - \delta\lambda \, \boldsymbol{q} = -\boldsymbol{R}\). It is solved with one factorization and two substitutions,

(4)#\[\delta \boldsymbol{u}_R = -\boldsymbol{K}_T^{-1} \boldsymbol{R}, \qquad \delta \boldsymbol{u}_q = \boldsymbol{K}_T^{-1} \boldsymbol{q}, \qquad \delta \boldsymbol{u} = \delta \boldsymbol{u}_R + \delta\lambda \, \delta \boldsymbol{u}_q\]

and the increment becomes \((\Delta \boldsymbol{u} + \delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda + \delta\lambda)\), with \(\delta\lambda\) from the constraint:

  • Riks, 'arc_length_riks': the constraint is linearized at the current increment, \(g + \langle (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}, \delta\lambda) \rangle = 0\), which gives

    (5)#\[\delta\lambda = -\frac{g + \langle (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_R, 0) \rangle}{\langle (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1) \rangle}\]

    This is Newton’s method applied to the system of the equilibrium equations augmented with the constraint, with quadratic convergence when \(\boldsymbol{K}_T\) is the exact Jacobian and is updated at every iteration (Crisfield 1991, Sec. 9.3.2, p. 272). Riks (1979, Secs. 3.1 to 3.3) augments the equilibrium equations with a linear equation, the plane normal to the tangent of the path at the last converged point at the distance \(\Delta s\) from it, and solves them by Newton’s method with quadratic convergence; with \(g = 0\) in (5), the constraint is the updated normal plane of Ramm (1981, p. 75); Crisfield (1991, Sec. 9.3.2.2, Eq. (9.29), pp. 274-275) attributes the mismatch term to Schweizerhof and Wriggers and to Riks. With it, each iteration satisfies the linearized constraint about the current increment, and the iterates return to the sphere of (1) as they converge.

  • Crisfield, 'arc_length_crisfield': the spherical constraint is solved exactly for \(\delta\lambda\). With \(\boldsymbol{v} = \Delta \boldsymbol{u} + \delta \boldsymbol{u}_R\), it is the quadratic equation

    (6)#\[a \, \delta\lambda^2 + b \, \delta\lambda + c = 0, \qquad a = \langle (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1) \rangle, \quad b = 2 \langle (\boldsymbol{v}, \Delta\lambda), (\delta \boldsymbol{u}_q, 1) \rangle, \quad c = \langle (\boldsymbol{v}, \Delta\lambda), (\boldsymbol{v}, \Delta\lambda) \rangle - \Delta s^2\]

    whose roots, computed as \(q/a\) and \(c/q\) with \(q = -\frac{1}{2}(b + \mathrm{sign}(b) \sqrt{b^2 - 4 a c})\) to avoid cancellation, give two new increments of the length \(\Delta s\). The root whose increment makes the smallest angle with the current one, i.e. the largest inner product, is chosen, which avoids the “doubling back” along the path already traced (Crisfield 1981, p. 56). When the roots are complex, the linearized constraint (5) is used for that iteration.

The convergence criterion is that of the Newton-Raphson method, see Convergence criteria, at the current load factor \(\lambda + \Delta\lambda\), with the external force vector of the increment, \(\Delta\lambda \boldsymbol{F}_{ext}\), as an additional reference, which keeps the criterion meaningful where the path crosses a null load. The step also fails when \(\delta\lambda\) cannot be computed or is not finite, when the divergence checks of The Newton-Raphson method stop the iterations, and when the converged increment points against the tangent predictor of the step, \(\langle (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \Delta\lambda), (\Delta \boldsymbol{u}_{pred}, \Delta\lambda_{pred}) \rangle \le 0\): the previous converged point is also at the distance \(\Delta s\) from the last one, and with long steps or near a limit point the iterations may converge to it. The accepted points are thus on the side of the predictor of the hyperplane normal to it through the last converged point, as the points of the plane of Riks.

With an.modified_NR = True, the factorization of the tangent at the last converged point is used for the first an.compute_every_n iterations and the tangent is then updated every an.compute_every_n iterations; with an.kT_initial_state = False, \(\boldsymbol{K}_{C0}\) replaces the tangent of the initial state at the first step.

Step length and termination#

After each converged step, the arc length is multiplied by \(\sqrt{I_d/n_{it}}\), with \(n_{it}\) the number of corrections of the step and \(I_d\) = DESIRED_NUM_ITER \(= 4\), bounded by MIN_GROWTH_FACTOR \(= 0.5\) and MAX_GROWTH_FACTOR \(= 1.5\), at most 1 after a step that was repeated, and limited by the larger of an.maxInc and the first arc length. The square root is the rule of Ramm (1981, p. 76), who found that the ratio \(I_d/n_{it}\) itself, used by Crisfield (1981, Eq. (15)), makes the number of iterations oscillate from step to step. A step that fails is repeated from the same converged point and predictor direction with the arc length multiplied by INC_CUT_FACTOR \(= 0.5\).

When a converged step crosses the load factor 1.0, from \(\lambda < 1\) to \(\lambda + \Delta\lambda > 1\), it is replaced by Newton-Raphson iterations at \(\lambda = 1\) exactly, from the predictor \(t \Delta \boldsymbol{u}\) with \(t = (1 - \lambda)/\Delta\lambda\); when they do not converge, the step is repeated with a shorter arc length. The analysis stops when the load factor 1.0 is reached, when the cumulative arc length reaches an.maxArcLength, 18 by default, when the arc length falls below an.minInc, after MAX_NUM_STEPS \(= 1000\) converged steps, or when the tangent stiffness matrix at the last converged point is singular. A path whose load factor never reaches 1.0, e.g. the post-buckling path of a structure whose load is beyond its limit load and does not recover, ends at an.maxArcLength: the attribute bounds the length of the path traced.

Choice between the two methods#

Both methods trace the same path, with the same predictor, step control and convergence criterion. Riks’ method is Newton’s method on the augmented system, with quadratic convergence and a linear equation for \(\delta\lambda\) that always has a solution while its denominator is not null. Crisfield’s method keeps the iterates on the sphere of radius \(\Delta s\) and needs the choice between two roots, with the fall-back to the linearized constraint when they are complex. tests/test_solver_arc_length.py checks both on analytic problems with exact tangents: the limit points of the shallow von Mises truss, snap-through and snap-back, the quadratic convergence, the step cutting, the modified Newton-Raphson, the landing on the load factor 1.0, the independence of the units of forces and displacements, and a sharp peak and complex roots; tests/test_solver_arc_length_fung_kaplan.py traces the snap-through of the low sinusoidal arch of Fung and Kaplan (1952) through both limit points with BeamC elements.

Loads that depend on the configuration or on the load factor#

When calc_fext declares c, \(\boldsymbol{q}\) in the predictor and in the bordered system is the load vector of the current configuration, calc_fext(inc=1., c=c), i.e. \(-\partial \boldsymbol{R}/\partial \lambda\) at the iterate, whereas \(\boldsymbol{u}_{ref}\) remains the linear solution of the reference vector; with it, the iterations of Riks’ method keep their quadratic convergence under follower loads, see tests/test_solver_follower_loads.py. When calc_fext also declares lbd, \(\boldsymbol{q}\) is evaluated at the current configuration and load factor, together with the tangent stiffness matrix, i.e. once per update of the tangent, and \(\lambda \boldsymbol{q}\) is an additional reference of the convergence criterion, see Load- and configuration-dependent loads.
